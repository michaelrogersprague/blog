"Alas," said the mouse, "the whole world is growing smaller every day. At the beginning it was so big that I was afraid, I kept running and running, and I was glad when I saw walls far away to the right and left, but these long walls have narrowed so quickly that I am in the last chamber already, and there in the corner stands the trap that I must run into."

"You only need to change your direction," said the cat, and ate it up.

### Think. 
There is a fatalism around the progress of AI: "There is a year left until things really get out of hand." Many people, I am told, will die. "Our best chance is not much of a chance at all" Maybe we stop, maybe AI save us from itself. That these risks cannot be dismissed out of hands is, I must say, unconscionable. That said, the contours of this fear -- these warnings and doom -- are eminently and somewhat reassuringly human. I want to spend a few minutes on this theme: 
### Groupthink. 
There is a famous story of a professor who polled a group of students some 30 years ago on the probability that a nuclear weapon will be detonated in an American city in the next 10 years. The professor, who clearly shared the same view, proudly shared that all students were in agreement in the answer: "100%". At some level, this was doubly wrong. Wrong as a matter of fact: no bomb did, in fact, detonate. But also wrong as a matter of truth seeking. Let's ask why. 

Estimates of these type are highly correlated and prone to the subtle bias and social pressure that comes from a disparity in status or power (teacher/student, supplicant and master). Why would a professor fall for such an obvious trap? He might have been credulous. He might have assumed that his audience was credulous. Both of these are rather transparent attempts at persuasion, of which I am not entirely convinced. 
### Fear. 
I would suggest another, more interesting, and deeply human motivation. The professor feared this outcome, feared it the point of obsession. This fear came as a dark cloud in the back of his mind when light streams into the office at the start of the day, a day which unfurls ahead in all of its manifold possibility. The fear is a terminal diagnosis looming in the back of your mind. This fear created a cognitive dissonance -- the happy unperturbed world outside and the dark despair within. To share such a dark prediction is to try to bridge to gap between the internal and the external, the hope and the despair. It's telling your family you are sick, that you can no longer go one as before, that the smile cannot hold, the mask slips and now we must confront the fear together. The way to understand the person is to know their fear. They are inseparable.  They are, altogether, human.
### N=1 predictions. 
Beyond the (human) motivations, what to make of offering a prediction in the face of doom. Whether that be p(doom_nuclear) or p(doom_AI). Whether it be economic catastrophe, rebellion, war, or subjugation. What to make of it?

Doctors rarely give exact predictions. When confronting a terrifying prognosis, patients ask, what are my odds? what chance do you give me?  Setting aside cases of innumeracy, doctors know there is a significant individual variation even when the population average is known (with AI, this is hardly the case). They might fear that the patient takes actions contrary to their best interests in the face of a low probability of success -- the Iatrogenic potential (https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/2661032). Or they could fear being held liable to a promised outcome (you said it was only 1%!) Better to chose a higher probability to hedge, like 20%.

But I still do wonder: what are our odds? What chance do you give us?
